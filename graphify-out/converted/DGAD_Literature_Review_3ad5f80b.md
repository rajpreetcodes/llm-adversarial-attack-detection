| Zou et al. (2023) | Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models | Aligned chat models such as Vicuna and LLaMA-2-Chat were assumed to be protected by RLHF safety training. Prior adversarial examples were mostly hand-crafted, and automated attacks had not been shown to reach closed commercial systems. | Automatically find short adversarial suffixes that make aligned LLMs comply with harmful requests, and test whether one suffix generalises across many queries and across models. | AdvBench harmful behaviours and harmful strings; open-weight surrogates (Vicuna-7B/13B, LLaMA-2-7B-Chat); black-box targets ChatGPT, Bard and Claude via public interfaces. Synthetic attack generations. | Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG): a mix of greedy and gradient-based discrete token search that optimises a 20-token suffix on a white-box surrogate, extended to universal multi-prompt, multi-model suffixes; transfer measured on held-out models. | Universal suffixes reliably flip refusals into affirmative compliance on open models, and they transfer to proprietary API-only systems that were never touched during optimisation, showing black-box deployment alone gives little protection. | Needs white-box access to a surrogate model; the suffixes are low-fluency gibberish that perplexity filters catch easily; success drops against models with additional defences. | Defines the primary attack family our statistical channel (windowed perplexity) and the adversarial regression suite must catch; motivates reproducing GCG offline for evaluation. |
| Perez and Ribeiro (2022) | Ignore Previous Prompt: Attack Techniques For Language Models | GPT-3 applications built on few-shot prompting hide instructions inside the prompt and concatenate untrusted user text with them, with no separation between the two channels. | Give the first systematic account of prompt-based attacks on language models, focusing on goal hijacking and system prompt leakage. | GPT-3 (davinci) few-shot prompts across standard NLP tasks; hand-crafted injection strings. Manually curated attack inputs. | Manual construction of injected instructions appended to user input; qualitative and quantitative evaluation of hijacking and prompt-leaking success across tasks. | Plain injections such as 'Ignore previous prompt' reliably redirect model behaviour and extract hidden system prompts, without any optimisation or model access. | Small-scale, manual study on a single pre-RLHF model family; no defence evaluation; predates modern aligned chat models. | Establishes the direct prompt injection threat model our detection layer handles; informs test-case design for the injection corpora. |
| Liu et al. (2023) | AutoDAN: Generating Stealthy Jailbreak Prompts on Aligned Large Language Models | GCG-style suffixes are gibberish and therefore easy to flag with perplexity filtering. Hand-written jailbreaks are fluent but scarce and hard to scale. | Automatically generate jailbreak prompts that stay fluent and human-readable while remaining effective, evading statistical filters. | Harmful behaviour sets derived from AdvBench; open-weight aligned LLMs as targets; optional warm start from existing manual jailbreak prompts. Synthetic attack generations. | Hierarchical genetic algorithm that evolves prompts at sentence level and then refines at word level, preserving readability while optimising jailbreak success. | Produces fluent, stealthy jailbreaks with high success rates that bypass perplexity-based detection, which GCG cannot do; readability is preserved by the sentence-level search. | Needs repeated query access to the target model; evaluated mainly on open-weight models; per-attack generation cost is non-trivial. | Direct evidence that a statistical-only detector fails on fluent attacks; the core justification for the embedding-based semantic channel in DGAD. |
| Su (2024) | Enhancing Adversarial Attacks through Chain of Thought | Gradient-based attacks such as GCG optimise for an affirmative target string, but their transferability and universality across models remain limited. | Improve transferability and universality of gradient jailbreaks by recruiting the target model's chain-of-thought reasoning into the attack. | Aligned open-weight LLMs; harmful behaviour sets; Llama Guard used as the safety classifier for evaluation. Synthetic attack generations. | CoT-GCG: GCG-style gradient optimisation aimed at reasoning-triggering prefixes rather than affirmative targets, evaluated with Llama Guard judgements. | CoT-GCG outperforms vanilla GCG and standalone CoT prompting in transferability and universality, exposing systematic weaknesses in alignment for reasoning-triggered harmful content. | Single-author preprint with limited peer review; still depends on a white-box surrogate; success measurement inherits the failure modes of the Llama Guard judge. | Shows the attack side keeps evolving after each defence appears; motivates DGAD's continuous self-adversarial calibration loop rather than a fixed training set. |
| Inan et al. (2023) | Llama Guard: LLM-based Input-Output Safeguard for Human-AI Conversations | Content moderation APIs are closed, fixed to vendor taxonomies, and not designed for human-AI conversation; open safeguards were missing. | Build an open, LLM-based safeguard that classifies both prompts and responses as safe or unsafe across a customisable safety risk taxonomy. | Safety risk taxonomy with multiple harm categories; training on a mix of public red-teaming data (Anthropic hh-rlhf) and internal data. Labelled prompt-response pairs. | Instruction fine-tuning of LLaMA2-7B for safe/unsafe classification of inputs and outputs, with zero-shot and few-shot adaptation to new taxonomies and categories. | Matches or exceeds closed moderation APIs (OpenAI moderation, Perspective API) on benchmarks, and adapts to new harm categories with few examples. | English-centric; runs a 7B-parameter model per request, so latency and cost are high for always-on use; as an LLM it is itself vulnerable to prompt attacks. | Reference design and baseline for our escalation-stage LLM judge (Channel D); its cost profile motivates invoking the judge only on contested prompts. |
| Liu et al. (2023) | Formalizing and Benchmarking Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses | Prompt injection was widely reported in practice but had no formal definition, and proposed defences were evaluated ad hoc, if at all. | Give a formal definition of prompt injection and build a general framework to benchmark attacks and defences consistently. | Constructed benchmark pairing target NLP tasks with injected tasks across multiple datasets. Synthetic benchmark of task pairs. | Formal framework modelling the attack as injected instruction plus data concatenated with the target instruction; systematic evaluation of prevention-based and detection-based defences under this framework. | Attacks achieve high success across LLMs, and existing defences offer only limited protection, confirming a wide gap between attack capability and deployed mitigation. | Benchmark tasks are synthetic NLP tasks rather than live applications; centres on injection into the data channel and covers fewer attack variants than later work. | Supplies the formal threat model and benchmark methodology our evaluation harness mirrors for direct prompt injection. |
| Yi et al. (2023) | Benchmarking and Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks on Large Language Models | Indirect injection, where the payload hides in third-party data the model consumes (web pages, emails, documents), was far less studied than direct injection. | Build a benchmark for indirect prompt injection and measure how well black-box and white-box defences hold up against it. | BIPIA benchmark spanning five scenarios: email QA, web QA, table QA, summarisation and code QA. Constructed text tasks with embedded payloads. | Benchmark construction with indirect attacks placed in external content; evaluation of defences including delimiter-based prompt engineering and fine-tuning, split by white-box versus black-box access. | Deployed LLMs are broadly vulnerable to indirect injection; prompt-engineering defences help modestly, while the stronger defences require white-box access to the model. | Stronger defences need model internals, which API-only deployments do not have; benchmark scenarios are limited to five text tasks; defence gains trade off against task utility. | Directly shapes DGAD's canary tripwire for indirect injection and documents the limits of delimiter-style preprocessing we otherwise rely on. |
| Alon and Kamfonas (2023) | Detecting Language Model Attacks with Perplexity | Production LLM applications needed a cheap first-line filter against jailbreak prompts circulating in the wild, without touching the target model. | Detect adversarial prompts using perplexity under a reference language model, including a windowed variant for long inputs. | In-the-wild jailbreak prompts gathered from online collections, contrasted with benign prompts. Real-world collected attack prompts. | Score each prompt by perplexity under a reference LM and apply a threshold; for long prompts, compute perplexity over sliding windows and aggregate, so a short anomalous segment is not diluted. | Perplexity filtering catches many real attacks at near-zero cost, and the windowed variant handles long prompts much better than whole-sequence scoring. | Blind to fluent attacks such as AutoDAN; a single global threshold forces a hard trade between detection rate and false positives; depends on reference LM quality. | The foundation of DGAD's statistical channel (Channel A); its documented blindness to fluent text is why Channel A never decides alone on ambiguous scores. |
| Jain et al. (2023) | Baseline Defenses for Adversarial Attacks Against Aligned Language Models | After GCG, many defences were proposed informally, but nobody had measured which baseline techniques actually reduce attack success and at what false-positive cost. | Systematically evaluate baseline defences against optimisation-based attacks on aligned LLMs, reporting performance at controlled false-positive rates. | AdvBench-style harmful behaviours; GCG and related attack variants; benign prompts for false-positive budgets. Synthetic attacks plus benign corpora. | Evaluation harness comparing perplexity filtering (including windowed), paraphrasing, retokenisation, LLM-based detection and adversarial training, each measured under fixed FPR budgets. | Windowed perplexity, paraphrasing and retokenisation cut attack success substantially; adversarial training helps most but is expensive; every defence forces the attacker to adapt. | Evaluation centres on white-box gradient attacks; adaptive attackers who know the defence can lower its success; some defences add real compute overhead. | Source of the windowed perplexity method for Channel A and of the FPR-budget evaluation protocol used in our harness. |
| Zizzo et al. (2025) | Adversarial Prompt Evaluation: Systematic Benchmarking of Guardrails Against Prompt Input Attacks on LLMs | A fast-growing ecosystem of guardrail products and models existed with no consistent comparison across attack styles and benign traffic. | Benchmark guardrail defences systematically across a broad set of malicious and benign datasets to find what actually works. | Broad collection of malicious jailbreak and injection datasets plus benign prompt sets; fifteen defences including Azure Prompt Shield and Meta Prompt Guard. Public benchmark corpora. | Unified benchmarking harness scoring each defence across attack families and benign sets, analysing score distributions and threshold behaviour rather than single accuracy numbers. | No defence dominates across the board; score-based detectors are constrained by a single global threshold, so raising detection rate raises false positives on unusual-but-benign prompts with it. | A snapshot of defences at one point in time; attackers are not fully adaptive within the benchmark; results depend on the datasets selected. | Quantifies why single-threshold detectors underperform; the direct motivation for calibrated scoring and disagreement-based escalation in DGAD. |
| Li and Liu (2024) | InjecGuard: Benchmarking and Mitigating Over-defense in Prompt Injection Guardrail Models | Prompt injection guard models were optimising detection rate while quietly blocking benign prompts that merely contain attack-associated trigger words (over-defence). | Benchmark the over-defence problem in guardrail models and mitigate it without sacrificing detection accuracy or deployability. | NotInject, a new benign set built to contain trigger words; existing prompt injection benchmarks. Curated benign and adversarial prompt corpora. | Fine-tuning encoder models (DeBERTa family) with the proposed MOF strategy to reduce over-defence; benchmark evaluation across injection sets and the NotInject benign set. | InjecGuard reaches accuracy competitive with general-purpose LLM judges while staying lightweight enough for low-latency deployment, and over-defence is measurable and reducible once it is tested for explicitly. | Focused on direct injection; performance varies on out-of-distribution attacks; English-only evaluation. | Baseline and design reference for DGAD's embedding classifier (Channel B); we adopt its practice of tracking over-defence FPR on a NotInject-style benign set separately. |
| Xie et al. (2024) | GradSafe: Detecting Jailbreak Prompts for LLMs via Safety-Critical Gradient Analysis | Existing detectors either need extra fine-tuning, paid LLM judges, or handcrafted rules; a training-free signal tied to the model's own safety behaviour was missing. | Detect jailbreak prompts by analysing gradients induced on safety-critical parameters, without fine-tuning the target model. | Jailbreak and benign prompt benchmarks including ToxicChat and XSTest; open-weight models for gradient access. Public benchmark corpora. | Pair each prompt with a compliant response prefix, backpropagate, slice gradients of safety-critical parameters; GradSafe-Zero uses cosine similarity to reference slices, GradSafe-Adapt trains a small logistic head. | Detects jailbreaks effectively and outperforms Llama Guard on benchmarks such as ToxicChat, with GradSafe-Zero requiring no training at all. | Requires white-box access to weights and backpropagation, so closed API-only models are out of reach; tied to assumptions about which parameter slices carry safety signal. | A strong contrast case for DGAD's black-box constraint: it shows what white-box signals buy and why a model-agnostic detector cannot depend on them. |
| Hackett et al. (2025) | Bypassing LLM Guardrails: An Empirical Analysis of Evasion Attacks against Prompt Injection and Jailbreak Detection Systems | Guardrail systems were being deployed and benchmarked as static classifiers, but almost nobody attacked the guardrails themselves with adaptive evasion. | Empirically measure whether prompt injection and jailbreak detection systems can be evaded by character-level obfuscation and adversarial ML techniques. | Six guardrail systems including commercial offerings; established prompt injection and jailbreak attack corpora as base payloads. Public attack corpora plus live guardrail endpoints. | Apply character-injection and obfuscation techniques alongside adversarial machine learning evasion to attack payloads, and measure detection before and after transformation across the six systems. | Evasion reaches up to 100% success in certain attack configurations; guardrails are themselves an attack surface facing the same optimisation pressure as the LLMs they protect. | Covers a fixed set of six systems; some obfuscation tricks can be patched once known; impact on benign-side false positives is studied less than evasion success. | The direct justification for DGAD's self-adversarial calibration loop and the adversarial regression test tier that attacks our own detector in CI. |
