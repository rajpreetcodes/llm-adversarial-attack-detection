Literature Review
Adversarial Attack Detection for Large Language Models (LLMs)

# 1. Introduction
A twenty-token string of optimised text is enough to make a safety-trained language model answer questions it was built to refuse [1]. Since that result appeared in 2023, adversarial input has moved from a research curiosity to an operational threat for any product that routes user text into a large language model (LLM). Customer support bots, coding assistants, and document pipelines all share the same seam: natural language is the interface, and it is also the attack vector.
This review surveys the research that defines the threat and the defences raised against it. Thirteen papers, published between 2022 and 2025, are organised into four themes: automated jailbreak generation, prompt injection, detection-based defences and guard models, and the benchmarking and evasion of guardrails. The purpose is to map what the field has established and to locate exactly where it falls short, because those shortcomings set the agenda for the detection system this project proposes.
# 2. Thematic Review
## 2.1 Automated Jailbreak Generation
The modern study of LLM attacks starts with Zou et al. [1], whose Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack searches for a short adversarial suffix that pushes an aligned model towards an affirmative answer instead of a refusal. The suffixes are universal, meaning one string works across many harmful queries, and they transfer: suffixes tuned on Vicuna and LLaMA-2-Chat jailbroke ChatGPT, Bard, and Claude through their public APIs, with no access to those systems during the attack.
Fluency was GCG's weak point. Its suffixes read as gibberish, so statistical filters catch them, and AutoDAN [3] exists precisely to close that hole: a hierarchical genetic algorithm evolves jailbreak prompts at sentence level and then word level, producing readable text that keeps its jailbreak effect while slipping past perplexity filters. Su's CoT-GCG [4] takes a different path. Rather than optimising for an affirmative target, it optimises for triggers that recruit the model's chain-of-thought reasoning, and it reports stronger transferability and universality than vanilla GCG when judged by Llama Guard [5]. Read together, these papers show jailbreak construction has become an optimisation problem, not a craft, and each new variant is designed around the defence that caught the previous one.
## 2.2 Prompt Injection: Attack Characterisation and Benchmarks
Jailbreaks attack the model's alignment directly. Prompt injection attacks the application wrapped around the model. Perez and Ribeiro [2] gave the first systematic account in 2022, showing that GPT-3 few-shot applications can be goal-hijacked, or made to leak their hidden system prompts, with inputs as plain as 'Ignore previous prompt'. No optimisation is needed, because the application concatenates trusted instructions with untrusted text and the model cannot reliably separate the two.
Liu et al. [6] later gave the attack a formal definition and a benchmark, evaluating prevention-based and detection-based defences across paired target and injected tasks. Their headline finding is uncomfortable: deployed defences blunt the attacks but do not stop them. Yi et al. [7] pushed the same question into the indirect setting, where the payload hides in data the model consumes, such as a web page, an email, or a table. Their BIPIA benchmark covers five such scenarios, and their defence experiments show delimiter-style prompt engineering helps only modestly, while the stronger defences demand white-box access that API-only deployments do not have.
## 2.3 Detection-Based Defences and Guard Models
The cheapest and oldest detector is perplexity. Alon and Kamfonas [8] proposed flagging prompts whose perplexity under a reference language model crosses a threshold, with a windowed variant for long inputs, and Jain et al. [9] put this and other baselines through a systematic evaluation at controlled false-positive budgets. Windowed perplexity works because gradient-optimised suffixes are locally improbable text, and it costs almost nothing to run. Its blindness is equally well documented: AutoDAN-style fluent attacks pass straight through it [3].
Learned detectors cover that blind spot. InjecGuard [11] fine-tunes a DeBERTa-family encoder on curated benign and adversarial corpora and reaches accuracy comparable to an LLM judge at a fraction of the latency. The same paper introduces the NotInject set to measure over-defence, the habit of guard models blocking benign prompts that merely contain attack-flavoured words, and shows the problem shrinks once it is tested for explicitly. GradSafe [12] skips fine-tuning altogether: it reads the gradients a prompt induces on safety-critical parameters when paired with a compliant response, and beats Llama Guard on ToxicChat and XSTest, but only where model weights are open to backpropagation. Llama Guard itself [5], an instruction-tuned LLaMA-2-7B classifying prompts and responses against a safety taxonomy, proved a single open model can rival closed moderation APIs, at the price of running a 7B-parameter model on every request.
## 2.4 Benchmarking Guardrails and Adaptive Evasion
Two recent studies measure how this patchwork holds up under pressure. Zizzo et al. [10] benchmarked fifteen defences across a broad set of malicious and benign datasets and found no winner. Score-based detectors are hostage to a single global threshold, so pushing recall up drags false positives on unusual-but-benign prompts up with it. Hackett et al. [13] then attacked the guardrails themselves, applying character-level obfuscation and adversarial machine learning evasion to six prompt injection and jailbreak detection systems, including commercial products, and reached up to 100% evasion in some configurations. A guardrail, it turns out, is just another model, exposed to the same optimisation pressure as the LLM behind it.
# 3. Critical Analysis
Read side by side, the offence and defence literatures do not agree with each other, and the disagreement is informative. Jain et al. [9] report that windowed perplexity cuts GCG success sharply at modest false-positive budgets, yet AutoDAN [3] was built the same year precisely because that defence exists. A detector tuned to catch [1] is blind to [3] by construction. Zizzo et al. [10] test defences against fixed attack corpora and conclude that no guardrail dominates. Hackett et al. [13], testing adaptively, find something worse: the guardrails do not merely underperform, they can be driven to zero. Static benchmarking flatters defences.
Methodological inconsistency makes cross-paper comparison harder than it should be. Attack success is judged by Llama Guard in [4], by string matching in [1], and by bespoke classifiers elsewhere, and each judge carries its own failure modes. False-positive behaviour gets far less attention than detection rate: of the detection papers, only InjecGuard [11] builds a dedicated benign set for over-defence, and latency or cost per prompt is rarely reported at all, even though those numbers decide whether a defence is deployable. GradSafe [12] posts strong benchmark scores but assumes white-box access, which excludes exactly the proprietary, API-only models that dominate production and that GCG's transfer results [1] proved vulnerable.
# 4. Research Gap Summary
Across the thirteen studies, six unresolved issues recur. Together they mark the space where a new contribution can still be made.
- Single-signal dependence. Every deployed-style defence relies on one detection strategy, so each is effective against one attack family and weak outside it: perplexity filters miss fluent attacks [3][8], embedding classifiers inherit their training distribution [11], and no work combines complementary signals beyond averaging or a fixed threshold [10].
- Static evaluation. Defences are benchmarked against frozen attack corpora. Adaptive evasion aimed at the detector itself is absent from training and evaluation, and when tested it succeeds at rates up to 100% [13].
- White-box dependence. The strongest detection signals, such as safety-critical gradients in [12], need model weights, which excludes the proprietary API-only models most deployments actually use.
- Cost-blind judging. LLM judges give interpretable verdicts but run a multi-billion-parameter model per request [5], and no existing framework optimises when a judge is worth its cost.
- Under-measured over-defence. False positives on unusual-but-benign prompts are rarely benchmarked; only [11] builds a dedicated benign set for it.
- Untreated detector disagreement. No surveyed work treats disagreement between independent detectors as information, rather than noise to be averaged away. The false-agreement case, where every cheap detector is fooled in the same direction, is unaddressed.
# 5. Link to the Present Study
The proposed system, DGAD (Disagreement-Gated Adaptive Detection), is assembled directly out of these gaps. From [8] and [9] it takes windowed perplexity as a cheap statistical channel, and from [10] and [11] an embedding-based classifier, with per-channel calibration applied before any comparison so that scores are commensurable. The core decision rule is new: instead of averaging channel scores under one global threshold, the failure mode quantified in [10], DGAD treats disagreement between calibrated channels as the routing signal. Agreement resolves immediately as pass or block. Only contested prompts are escalated to a behavioural probe and an LLM judge, which answers the cost problem of always-on judges documented in [5]. The 100% evasion results of [13] are handled by a self-adversarial calibration loop that searches for inputs fooling all cheap detectors in the same direction and folds them back into training data, and by an adversarial regression suite that attacks the detector itself. The whole pipeline stays black-box and model-agnostic, deliberately forgoing the white-box signals of [12] so it can sit in front of any LLM, open or proprietary. Over-defence is measured on a NotInject-style benign set, following [11], rather than being discovered in production.
# 6. Conclusion
The literature tells a consistent story across four years. Attacks are automated, transferable, and increasingly fluent. Individual defences each cover one attack family and fail outside it, and static benchmarks overstate how well the patchwork holds. The strongest evidence, from [10] and [13], is that the binding constraint is not any single detector's accuracy but the rigidity of a lone signal under one threshold facing an adaptive opponent. That constraint, and the unresolved gaps catalogued above, define the requirements for a deployable detector. The next section presents the methodology of the proposed DGAD pipeline, which is designed to meet them.
